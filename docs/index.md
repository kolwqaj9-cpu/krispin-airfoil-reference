## 1. Summary of rocket-model tests at zero lift of the Northrop MX-775B missile configuration from Mach numbers of 0.9 to 1.8

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20090026523>

A NACA research memorandum presenting free-flight rocket-model drag results for the MX-775B missile configuration in the transonic and low-supersonic range. The report compares body-fin configurations at zero lift, with the model drawings covering the horizontal-fin and vertical-fin sections and the support details, and gives the measured drag behavior of small-body and large-body variants across Mach 0.9 to 1.8. Area distributions and equivalent-radius plots for both models accompany the drag data, so the aerodynamicist can relate the measured drag rise to the cross-sectional development of each configuration.

## 2. Summary of section data on trailing-edge high-lift devices

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20050080791>

The reference compendium of two-dimensional section data for plain, split, and slotted flaps on NACA airfoil sections. Its durable findings: the maximum lift coefficient generally increases with flap deflection up to about 60° to 70°, except for the largest flaps, which help only at small deflections; for 25-percent-chord flaps on the NACA 65-210 section, increasing the slot-lip extension does not raise the maximum lift coefficient beyond the values already reached; and for plain and flapped airfoils alike, maximum lift coefficients fall slowly as the position of minimum pressure moves rearward, regardless of thickness ratio. The report also documents that the maximum-lift increment from a 0.20c flap is essentially independent of Reynolds number across the tested range, that roughness penalizes the 230-series sections more than the 6-series without reversing the ranking, and that thin-airfoil theory cannot be expected to predict split-flap loads because a deflected split flap presents a thick, blunt body rather than a thin mean line. Optimum flap size and deflection both grow with airfoil thickness.

## 3. NACA conference on aerodynamic problems of transonic airplane design

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19790079919>

The conference record captures how the NACA framed transonic drag when jet aircraft first ran into it. The central example: for a representative modern jet airplane with a wing loading of about 50 pounds per square foot at 30,000 feet, an increase of about 100 miles per hour — roughly Mach 0.25 — above the speed where the drag rise starts would cut range by about 75 percent, while delaying or eliminating the drag rise would buy the same 100 mph for only about a 10-percent range loss, or 200 mph for about 20 percent. The conference papers also report that reducing thickness ratio moves the Mach number of the drag rise up by about 0.015 to 0.02 — roughly 10 to 15 mph — for each percent of thickness removed, and present the effects of sweepback and aspect ratio on the drag-rise Mach number from high-speed tunnel and free-fall test series.

## 6. Vortex Flow Aerodynamics, volume 1

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19860017718>

The first volume of the NASA conference publication on vortex flow aerodynamics, collecting the 1985-era state of the art on leading-edge vortex formation, vortex breakdown, vortex-body interaction, and the computational and experimental methods used to treat them. The volume is a standard entry point for slender-wing and high-angle-of-attack work: it gathers the fundamental papers on separated vortex flows that later delta-wing, strake, and forebody-control designs build on, and its survey material remains the common reference frame for the subject.

## 7. The NACA variable-density wind tunnel

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930091490>

The design report for the NACA variable-density tunnel — the pressurized facility that produced much of the classic airfoil data of the 1920s and 1930s. The report describes the pressure tank, the drive and compression procedure, the balance cradle with its knife-edge seats, the drag linkages and torque tubes that restrain the cradle, and the model mounting and alignment procedure, including the drilling jig used to mount airfoil models and the inclinometer method for setting chord angle. The return passage, damping screens, exit cone, and the antivortex vanes behind the propeller are all described, along with the pressure distribution measurements used to qualify the flow. The tunnel's working sections and balance geometry explain both the strengths and the wall corrections of the airfoil data published from it.

## 8. NACA Report 824 — Summary of Airfoil Data

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930090976>

The classic summary of NACA airfoil section data, later expanded into the book "Theory of Wing Sections." The report explains the method of combining a mean line with a thickness distribution to form cambered sections, tabulates the aerodynamic characteristics of the NACA families — the four-digit, five-digit, 6-series, and 7-series sections — and presents the data in two coordinated curve sets: the conventional plot of lift, drag, and moment against angle of attack corrected to aspect ratio 6, and the deduced characteristics of the infinite-span section with profile drag coefficient, angle of attack, and quarter-chord moment coefficient plotted against lift coefficient. The worked example for a rectangular Clark Y wing of aspect ratio 8 shows the intended use: pick the lift coefficient as the independent variable, then read off the angle of attack, drag coefficient, maximum lift-to-drag ratio, and center of pressure from the charts. The sample derivation of the NACA 65,3-818 section ordinates illustrates the mean-line and thickness combination arithmetic in full.

## 9. Application of circulation control technology to airframe noise reduction

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20030065818>

A study of circulation control — tangential blowing over a rounded trailing edge — applied to airframe noise. The interest is twofold: circulation control can replace conventional high-lift flaps with a pneumatic system whose geometry generates less airframe noise, and the blowing itself can be scheduled to trade lift augmentation against acoustic output. The report documents the noise and lift behavior of the configuration and places circulation control among the candidate technologies for quieter approach trajectories.

## 10. Experimental studies of low-pressure turbine flows and flow control

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20120016011>

NASA/CR-2012-217656 documents boundary-layer measurements on a low-pressure turbine cascade, with attention to separation, transition, and reattachment under varying free-stream turbulence. The measurement program is specified precisely: a single-sensor boundary-layer probe for near-wall profiles and a cross-wire probe (TSI model 1243-T1.5) for wall-normal velocity and turbulent shear stress at the downstream stations, with 25 points per profile beginning 1 mm from the wall; turbulent shear stress uncertainty is stated at 10 percent. The local free-stream velocities at the upstream stations closely followed a Falkner-Skan wedge-flow distribution. Under high free-stream turbulence — nearly isotropic, with rms intensities of about 8.8, 8.9, and 8.3 percent in the three directions — separation and reattachment shift relative to the low-turbulence cases, and small differences in streamwise pressure gradient and turbulence length scale account for the differences in reattachment location between this study and earlier ones. The report is a reference dataset for transition-model validation in low-pressure turbine design.

## 11. NASA Hybrid Thermally Efficient Core (HyTEC) Phase 2 annual report

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20260007509>

The HyTEC Phase 2 unlimited-rights annual report covering February 2025 to February 2026. The report tracks the core-engine demonstrator work, including the Phase 1 CLASSIC rig builds: an aero build to set engine-representative loading matching the bow wave and airfoil pressure distribution, a traverse build, and an optical/sniffer build, progressing through flow-field characterization, bow-wave measurement, and gas-ingestion analysis at the combustor–first-stage-nozzle interface. In this phase the rig uses a single-gap geometry representative of takeoff conditions and examines variable prescribed purge flow rates in the gap; modular components allow simplified swapping between builds. The document carries the NASA STI level-of-review statement and remains subject to revision as analysis proceeds.

## 12. Some applications of the quasi vortex-lattice method in steady and unsteady aerodynamics

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19760021096>

A methods paper applying the quasi vortex-lattice method to steady and unsteady problems. The presentation begins from the two-dimensional downwash equation, transforms the integral, and reduces it to a finite sum through the midpoint trapezoidal rule, then extends the same construction to lifting surfaces in steady and oscillatory motion. The value of the paper is its compact treatment: it shows how far a vortex-lattice formulation with simplified wake modeling can be pushed for forces, moments, and unsteady responses before a fuller panel or field method is needed.

## 13. Extended applications of the vortex lattice method

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19760021078>

A companion methods paper extending the vortex lattice method beyond its classical steady, planar-wing setting — to nonplanar geometries, interfering surfaces, and configurations where the wake model dominates the answer. Together with section 12 it forms a compact reference pair for engineers who still use lattice methods for early-stage configuration screening.

## 14. CEAS/AIAA/ICASE/NASA Langley international forum on aeroelasticity and structural dynamics 1999

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19990050911>

The proceedings of the 1999 international forum on aeroelasticity and structural dynamics hosted at NASA Langley — the reference venue for flutter prediction, gust and buffet response, aeroservoelastic coupling, and computational structural dynamics as they stood at the end of the 1990s. The papers remain a useful checkpoint for validating aeroelastic analysis tools against the test cases the community agreed on.
